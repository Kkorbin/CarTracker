Buying in Arizona

What you're walking into · ← back to listings

The two things most likely to cost you

There is no cooling-off period in Arizona. No three-day right to cancel, no returns. The moment you sign, the car is yours. Any "you can bring it back" reassurance is worth nothing unless it is written into the contract as an express right of rescission.

You do, however, get a 15-day / 500-mile implied warranty. Under A.R.S. § 44-1267, a dealer-sold used car carries an implied warranty of merchantability for 15 days or 500 miles, whichever comes first. You must give the dealer two chances to repair before seeking a refund, and you may owe up to $25 per repair attempt. An "As Is" box does not override this for covered sales. Drive the car hard in the first two weeks — that is the window.

Before you go

  • Get pre-approved for financing. Your credit union or bank rate is the number to beat. Dealers can mark up the rate they're offered by the lender and keep the difference, so arriving without a rate means negotiating blind.
  • Line up insurance. You need proof of coverage to drive off. Get a quote on the specific VIN beforehand — a 2018 CR-V and a 2022 Forester do not cost the same to insure, and it can shift which car is cheaper to own.
  • Book an independent pre-purchase inspection. Roughly $100–$200 at a shop of your choosing, never the selling dealer's. The Arizona Attorney General recommends it outright. On a car with a reported accident it is not optional.
  • Decide your walk-away number before you arrive, as an out-the-door total, and write it down.

How the money actually works here

  • Sales tax follows the dealer's city, not yours. Phoenix is 8.6%, Gilbert and Mesa 8.3%, Peoria 8.1%, Scottsdale 8.0%. On a $22,000 car the gap between Scottsdale and Phoenix is about $132 — small, but it is real and it is not negotiable, so factor it into which car is genuinely cheapest.
  • Doc fees are not meaningfully capped. Sources disagree on whether a $499 statutory cap exists; most say Arizona has none, and the state average runs around $569. Treat it as negotiable-adjacent: dealers rarely remove it outright, but they will discount the car to offset it. Ask for the out-the-door price and let them decide where to take it from.
  • Vehicle License Tax is based on the car's original MSRP, not what you pay — 60% of MSRP, reduced 16.25% per year since new, taxed at $2.89 per $100. Buying a well-equipped car cheaply does not reduce it. Expect roughly $200–$275/year on these.
  • Emissions testing. Phoenix is in Area A. The five newest model years are exempt, so for 2026 that means 2021 and older must pass a test (about $17, every two years) before it can be registered. Ask whether the dealer has already done it.

The finance office

This is the highest-pressure room in the building, and it comes after you have mentally already bought the car. Everything below is optional.

  • Add-ons to decline. The Attorney General specifically names window etching, nitrogen-filled tires and paint protection. Window tint is the most common Arizona add-on, appearing on roughly 47% of itemised quotes at an average of $549.
  • Pre-installed add-ons. If something is already on the car, ask for it to be removed or discounted. "It's already installed" is a sales position, not a fact about your obligations.
  • Extended warranty / service contract. Never required for financing. If you want one you can buy it later, usually cheaper, and you can always decline today.
  • GAP insurance only matters if you are financing most of the car. Your own insurer often sells it for less.
  • Negotiate the price, never the monthly payment. A payment can be made to look good by stretching the term — 72 months at a nice-sounding number costs far more than 60 at a worse-sounding one.

At the car

  • Check open recalls — each listing on the tracker does this live against NHTSA. Outstanding recall work is fixed free at a franchise dealer; get it done before you buy, or get it in writing.
  • Test drive on the freeway. Around the block hides wind noise, vibration at speed, alignment pull, and how the transmission behaves warm.
  • Cold start it. If the engine is already warm when you arrive, that may be deliberate. Ask to come back to a cold car.
  • Tires: tread depth and the four-digit date code on the sidewall. A set is around $800, and old tires are a real negotiating lever even with good tread.
  • Match the VIN on the dash, the door jamb sticker and the paperwork. Check the title status printed on the buyer's order.
  • Get the out-the-door figure in writing before agreeing to anything — itemised, with every fee named.
